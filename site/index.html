    What is measured, and what was made

    The talk, the topics and the marks on each word come from the Switchboard corpus: telephone calls between strangers, recorded in 1991 and 1992 and annotated by people. The sentence types and the kinds of disfluent word were made for this piece. Each is produced by a fixed rule, written out below, so the same files always give the same picture. No utterance was labelled case by case, by a person or by an AI model. One thing was not made by rule: the grouping of topics under umbrellas.

    From the corpus, as annotated

    The words, topics, dates and annotationsUsed as they are
    • The words of each call and who said them: the Switchboard transcripts.
    • Topic: the prompt the two callers were given. It is not a coding of what they went on to say.
    • Date of the call.
    • Disfluency marks on words: filled pauses, editing terms, repairs (with the part that gets redone marked off) and cut-off words, from the corpus's disfluency annotation.
    • A parse of each sentence, from the Penn Treebank release of Switchboard. The sentence types below are read off these parses.
    • A dialog act for each utterance: what it is doing, such as a statement, a question or a backchannel. The corpus has 42 kinds; their names are shortened here.

    Made for this piece, by rule

    The five sentence typesThe colors of every bar

    The corpus has no such category. A script gives every utterance one type, in two steps.

    Step 1: read the parse. Each sentence in the utterance has a parse in the corpus. Its top label decides a first type:

    Complete sentenceThe parse is a clause (top label S, SQ, SBARQ or SINV) that the annotators did not mark as unfinished. A clause that opens with "because" and has a full sentence after it counts too.
    Unfinished clauseThe parse is a clause the annotators marked as unfinished (the tag -UNF, outside any repair).
    Response tokenThe parse is an interjection (top label INTJ): "yeah", "uh-huh", "right".
    FragmentAnything else: a noun phrase, a prepositional phrase, or a clause opening with "if" or "when" that stands alone.

    Where an utterance holds more than one parsed sentence, it takes the type of the longest.

    Step 2: three corrections, applied in this order:

    1. An utterance of linking words only ("and", "but", "so", "or", "then", "because"), with or without an "uh" or "um", is a fragment.
    2. A fragment the annotators marked as abandoned is an unfinished clause: the speaker started something and dropped it ("what I-").
    3. A complete sentence is with a disfluency if it holds any repair or any disfluent word (the six kinds in the next entry). Otherwise it is fluent.

    Words are then counted by the type of the utterance they sit in.

    The step 2 corrections were added after a trial in which 30 utterances were coded by hand and compared with the script. The revised rules have not yet been checked against a full hand-coded sample.

    Disfluent words, and their six kindsThe thin column beside an umbrella; the colored words in a line of talk

    A word counts as disfluent if the corpus marks it as one of five things. The marks are the corpus's. The plain names, the choice of "you know" from among the discourse markers, and the split of filled pauses into two, are ours.

    Repeated or rewordedThe part of a repair that gets redone: the words a speaker says and then says again ("about, uh, about") or changes ("we were, I was"). The corpus calls this the reparandum.
    Editing termWords that flag a repair, nearly always "I mean".
    Cut-off wordA word broken off before its end, outside a repair.
    "You know"The two words, where the corpus marks them as a discourse marker. "You know" said as part of a sentence ("you know the one") is not counted. Other discourse markers, such as "well" and "like", are not counted either.
    "Uh" and "um"Filled pauses, split by which of the two was said.

    Rates are these words per 100 words spoken.

    The 650 callsWhich calls are in the piece

    This release of the corpus holds 1,155 calls. Only 650 of them come with parses, and without a parse a sentence cannot be given a type, so the piece shows those 650.

    The average callThe umbrella bars

    A bar's height is the mean number of words per call among the calls under that umbrella. Each segment is the mean number of those words that sit in one sentence type.

    The order of calls on the black screenWhy each topic looks like a hump

    Topics run in alphabetical order. Inside a topic the calls are sorted by length and dealt alternately to the left and the right, so the longest land in the middle. Bar heights share one scale within an umbrella, not across umbrellas.

    The lines of talkThe quotes beside a call in focus

    Each quote is chosen by rule from that call's own utterances of that type.

    • Response tokens: the wording used most often in the call.
    • The other four types: the utterance whose length in words is closest to the median for that type in the call. If two are equally close, the earlier one.

    A quote is typical in length only. It does not claim to be typical of what was said.

    The two strands and the cordThe single call

    Each bar is one utterance, as long as its number of words, placed in the order the transcript gives. The upper strand is the person who placed the call, the lower strand the person who answered. Where the other speaker comes in halfway through an utterance, it shows as two bars of the same color.

    Where a strand turns to cord, that person is waiting: the cord is as long as the words the other person says in the meantime. It is not silence. This release of the corpus has no timing, so the piece cannot show how long anything took, or where two people spoke at once.

    Most transcripts stop without a goodbye: only 172 of the 650 calls have a closing among their last few utterances. Where the bars stop, the corpus's transcript ends. The page has not cut anything off.

    An outlined bar is an utterance with no parse, and so no type.

    Made for this piece, by judgment

    The ten topic umbrellasWhich topics are grouped together

    The corpus lists 64 topic prompts among these calls and does not group them. LLM assisted semantic analysis of topic commonalities determined umbrella groupings with a human review and refining pass. A different grouping would move calls between bars.
